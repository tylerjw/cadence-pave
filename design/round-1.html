The two directions

—

Both carry the idea you liked in Halcyon: the shape of your day, drawn around the outside, where it costs nothing. They disagree about what shape that is — a lap, or a row of blocks. Metric and Route are gone; Route asked the screen for detail it does not have.

The argument for each

Liveries

every colour snapped to the 64-colour palette

The Pebble's palette is two bits per channel: every value is 00, 55, AA or FF. There is no #FC6719. Rather than fight that, I picked liveries whose brand colours land near palette entries anyway — Zwift's orange resolves to #FF5500, which is literally GColorOrange. Marks are drawn as original geometry in each brand's palette, not lifted logos.

The livery wall

—

Every fabric on one face, at whatever the zoom control is set to. Drop to 1× to see all eleven at life size — that is the honest test of which ones survive on a reflective screen.

The complication set

one wide slot, or two narrow

Each one is drawn twice: at the wide size it gets when it's the only complication, and the narrow size it gets when you run two. Nothing here needs a sub-pixel line or a chart.

Rules I designed to

One colour, one job

A livery may use white in more than one role, but no two roles that touch on screen may share a value — field, daylight, night and accent are now four distinct colours in every livery, and the secondary text colour is checked against its own field rather than inherited from the kit.

The marks drawn on the track — sunrise and sunset bars, the wheel, the lane paint, the stencil — no longer trust the palette at all. Each one resolves at draw time against the specific surfaces it crosses, picking whichever of the livery's colours holds up. That is what stops a white tick landing on a white track.

The screen, honestly

Reflective e-paper has no backlight in normal use, so contrast beats saturation. Every livery pins its field to either a very light or very dark value and never asks you to read mid-grey on mid-grey. Turn on E-paper sim in the control bar to see the muted, papery version — that is closer to the wrist than the vivid version.

Turn 64-colour off and on: the difference is small precisely because every colour was chosen on the palette grid in the first place.

Deliberately absent

  • No hands. At 202 ppi a swept hand is a staircase, and you asked to lean digital.
  • No hairlines. Nothing thinner than 2 px, no 1-px chapter rings, no dotted minute tracks.
  • No elevation profile, no ride stats. That's a head unit's job. This is a watch you happen to ride in.
  • No battery meter. Thirty days between charges is the best thing about this watch. A gauge you never need to look at is just a gauge in the way — so the slot went to the kit instead.
  • No rounded corners anywhere on the face. Every edge lands on the pixel grid.
  • One numeral face: Anton. Condensed enough that the digits get bigger everywhere — the auto-fit was hitting the width ceiling before, and now it hits the height ceiling instead. Small labels stay on Silkscreen, snapped to its 8 px design ladder.

Where the whimsy is hidden

Following the Brew's espresso zone — a joke that is also a functional scale — the cycling references here all do something:

  • Flamme rouge. The last hour before sunset burns accent-red on the day track. The 1 km kite, telling you how much light is left.
  • Start / finish. Midnight is a checkered block, top dead centre. Your day is one lap of a circuit.
  • The wheel. "Now" is a hub-and-tyre marker riding the track.
  • The swatch. A patch of the kit's own fabric — Mapei's cubes, Ineos's dithered drip, Visma's chest bands, Surly's check. One purely decorative block, different in every livery.
  • The race plate. The date complication is a number board with a punched border.

What came off the board

Metric put a ring in a square, which wasted all four corners and left an exergue too shallow to hold two complications at a readable size. It was the most watch-like of the four and the least useful.

Route died of resolution. The bike lane worked at 3× and dissolved at 1×: a lane stencil, chevrons and edge lines are a lot of small marks competing inside 52 px of width, and the screen does not have the detail to carry them. A pattern survives the pixel grid; a drawing of a thing mostly does not. That is the same lesson that killed the jersey.

The label that said nothing

There used to be a word next to the time reading DAY or NIGHT. It was naming the sun's phase — which the track already draws, in colour, at a glance. A label that only ever repeats the graphic beside it is noise, so it is gone.

What is left in that slot is a tag that stays silent until it has something to say: when the last hour of daylight begins, FLAMME ROUGE lights up in the accent colour, matching the segment burning on the track. The rest of the day, the slot is empty.

Second time zone, not GMT

The GMT complication is a named zone, not a fixed UTC offset — it shows a three-to-five letter city tag, the local time there, and a filled or hollow pip for whether it's day or night at the other end. It defaults to Paris, for reasons I hope are obvious in July.

Because sunrise and sunset are already drawn into every face, the Sun complication earns its slot by giving you the exact times plus a countdown, not a repeat of the graphic.
